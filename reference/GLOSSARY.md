## HVAC

### HVAC
**H**eating, **V**entilation, and **A**ir **C**onditioning. The whole system that heats, cools, and moves air through a home. Often used loosely to mean "the furnace + AC + ductwork" even though ventilation is technically its own function.

### Air filter
A replaceable pleated or fiberglass panel that traps dust, pollen, and debris before air reaches the equipment. Lives at the return-air side of the system. See [[HVAC-preventative-maintenance|the HVAC maintenance reference]] for change cadence.

### MERV rating
**M**inimum **E**fficiency **R**eporting **V**alue. A 1–16 scale rating how fine the particles a filter can capture. Higher = finer filtration but more airflow resistance.
- MERV 8 — basic residential (dust, pollen)
- MERV 11 — better residential (pet dander, fine dust)
- MERV 13 — good for allergies/smoke; near the top of what most home systems handle without strain
- MERV 14+ — usually commercial; can choke a residential system

### Return air
The path air takes *back* to the HVAC equipment to be heated or cooled. The filter almost always lives somewhere along this path — at the return grille (wall/ceiling) or at the equipment itself (blower compartment).

### Supply air
The conditioned (heated or cooled) air pushed *out* into the rooms through supply registers.

### Blower
The fan that moves air across the heat exchanger/evaporator coil and through the ducts. Often called the "indoor unit's heart."

### Evaporator coil
The cold indoor coil (in cooling mode) that absorbs heat from indoor air. Also dehumidifies as moisture condenses on it. Located inside, usually above the furnace or in an air handler.

### Condenser coil
The hot outdoor coil that releases heat pulled from inside the home. Located in the big outdoor unit (the "condensing unit").

### Fins
The thin aluminum blades covering the condenser (and evaporator) coils. They dramatically increase the coil's surface area so heat transfers efficiently to/from the air. Soft and easily bent — by lawnmowers, hail, pressure washers, or a careless bump.

### Fin comb (fin straightener)
An inexpensive plastic/metal comb with teeth sized to match a coil's fins-per-inch count. Drawn gently through bent fins to realign them. Restores airflow that bent fins choke off.

### Condenser pad
The concrete or composite slab the outdoor condensing unit sits on. Should be level — an out-of-level pad strains the compressor and can cause refrigerant oil to pool wrong.

### Condensate drain / drain line
The pipe that carries away water condensed from the air at the evaporator coil. A clogged condensate drain is a top cause of indoor water leaks and shutdowns. Covered in [[Condensate Drain Line|Lesson 0002]].

### Condensate
The water that condenses out of warm, humid indoor air when it hits the cold evaporator coil. In cooling mode, a typical system produces several gallons a day — all of it has to drain somewhere.

### Drain pan
The shallow tray under the evaporator coil that catches condensate and funnels it into the drain line. If it rusts, cracks, or tilts, water bypasses the drain and overflows.

### Cleanout T (T-vent)
The PVC T-fitting near the indoor air handler that gives you access to the drain line for inspection and flushing. It has a removable cap (often the tall vertical stub) — this is where you pour vinegar/bleach/water. Cap must go back on for the line to drain correctly.

### Float switch (overflow / safety switch)
A safety device in or near the drain pan. When backed-up condensate lifts a small float, the switch opens and **shuts down the system** (compressor + fans) to prevent water overflowing into the house. If your AC mysteriously won't turn on in summer, a tripped float switch from a clogged drain is a prime suspect.

### Algae / biofilm
The slimy biological growth (algae, mold, bacteria) that builds up inside a dark, wet condensate line and slowly narrows it until it clogs. The reason for routine flushing with vinegar or bleach.

### Heat pump
A single unit that *reverses* the refrigeration cycle to provide both cooling (summer) and heating (mild-winter climates). Moves heat rather than generating it — very efficient. Increasingly common; replaces the furnace + AC combo in many homes.

### Refrigerant
The fluid circulating inside the sealed system that absorbs and releases heat. **Handling refrigerant requires EPA certification and special equipment — not a DIY task.** If the system is low on refrigerant, there is a leak; a pro must find and repair it.

### Thermostat
The control that senses indoor temperature and signals the HVAC equipment to cycle on/off. The setpoint is the target temperature.

### Ductwork
The network of rigid or flexible ducts distributing supply air and collecting return air. Leaky ducts are a major hidden efficiency loss.

## Plumbing

### Drain bladder
A rubber bulb that attaches to a garden hose; expands inside a drain pipe and forces water jets out through the sides to clear buildup. The tool used in the user's recent sewer-drain clearing.

### Auger / plumber's snake
A flexible metal cable, hand- or motor-driven, that physically breaks through a clog in a drain line. Small hand-crank versions clear fixture drains (sinks, tubs); larger motorized versions clear the main sewer line.

### Sewer lateral
The underground pipe carrying waste from a home out to the city's sewer main (usually in the street). **The homeowner owns and is responsible for the lateral all the way to the connection point with the city main** — not just to the property line, in most municipalities. This is the pipe that, when clogged, causes the basement-floor drain backup the user experienced.

### Cleanout
A capped pipe (usually 4" diameter) giving direct access to the main sewer line — the entry point for augering, bladder-flushing, or camera inspection. Usually found outside within ~5–10 ft of the foundation, between the house and the street. Some homes have an indoor cleanout in the basement near where the line exits the foundation. **Locate yours before you have an emergency.**

### Main line (main drain)
The large pipe that all the home's fixture drains (sinks, toilets, tubs, washing machine) empty into. It runs from the home, through the sewer lateral, to the city main. A clog here backs up the lowest drain in the house (often a basement floor drain).

### Fixture drain
The drain at a single plumbing fixture — a sink, tub, toilet, or washing machine. Each has its own trap and is isolated from the main line until it joins the stack.

### P-trap
The U-shaped pipe under a sink (or curved section of a toilet) that holds a plug of water. The water seal blocks sewer gases from coming up through the drain into the home. Common collection point for sink clogs (hair, grease, jewelry).

### Hydro jetting
Clearing a drain line with high-pressure water (often 3,000–4,000 PSI) through a specialized nozzle. Cuts through roots and scours the pipe walls clean. Stronger than augering but almost always a **pro job** — requires equipment and can damage old/fragile pipes if misused.

### Root intrusion
Tree roots growing into a sewer line through joints, cracks, or loose connections — a leading cause of recurring main-line clogs, especially in older clay or cast-iron pipe. Mechanical clearing + chemical root killer is the preventive routine; pipe lining or replacement is the permanent fix.

### Foaming root killer
A preventive chemical treatment (copper sulfate or dichlobenil based) poured down a toilet to kill root growth inside the line. Applied annually *after* mechanical clearing, not as a substitute for it.

### Water meter leak indicator
A small dial, triangle, or star-shaped wheel on the face of the water meter that spins even when a tiny amount of water is moving. With all fixtures off, if it moves, there's a leak somewhere between the meter and the house. The single best whole-house leak test.

### Flapper (toilet)
The rubber seal at the bottom of a toilet tank that lifts when you flush. When it warps, mineral-fouls, or cracks, water silently leaks from the tank into the bowl 24/7 — the most common "hidden" toilet leak, and a top cause of mystery high water bills. Diagnosed with the dye test.

### Wax ring (toilet)
The wax seal between the base of a toilet and the floor flange. Blocks sewer gas and prevents leaks. When it fails, water seeps out around the toilet base with every flush — and eventually rots the subfloor. Replacement requires lifting the toilet.

### Fill valve (toilet)
The tall assembly in the toilet tank that refills the tank after a flush. Includes a float (cup or ball) that rises with the water level and shuts the valve off at the set height. When it fails, the tank either overflows into the overflow tube, won't fill, or hisses continuously without shutting off. The modern replacement is a Fluidmaster-style cup float valve — universal, ~$10, 10-minute swap.

### Flush valve (toilet)
The assembly in the center of the tank that releases water into the bowl when you flush — the overflow tube, the seat the flapper seals against, and the handle/lever mechanism. Larger and more involved to replace than the flapper or fill valve; usually only replaced when the seat is corroded/cracked or the overflow tube leaks.

### Overflow tube
The vertical pipe in the center of the flush valve, open at the top. If the fill valve fails and the tank overfills, water runs down the overflow tube into the bowl instead of flooding the bathroom — a safety path. If tank water is <em>constantly</em> running into the overflow tube, the fill valve isn't shutting off (or the float is set too high).

### Flapper chain
The short chain or link connecting the flush handle lever to the flapper. Length is critical: too tight and the flapper won't seat (constant leak); too loose and it doesn't lift enough to flush (weak flush). Adjust to give the flapper just enough slack to seat with the handle at rest.

### Float (toilet)
The buoyant component on the fill valve that rises with tank water level and triggers shutoff. Two common types: the older <strong>ball float</strong> (a hollow ball on an arm) and the modern <strong>cup float</strong> (a cylinder sliding up the fill valve shaft). Both adjust to set the tank's water level — typically via a screw on the valve or a squeeze-clip on the float.

### Floor flange (closet flange)
The metal or PVC ring bolted to the floor at the drain opening, to which the toilet is anchored by two closet bolts. The wax ring sits between the flange and the toilet horn. A broken or corroded flange is the hidden cause of a wobbly toilet and recurring wax-ring leaks — replacing it is a bigger job than a wax ring swap.

### Closet bolts
The two long bolts at the base of the toilet that anchor it to the floor flange. Hidden under decorative caps. Loose closet bolts cause a wobble that breaks the wax seal; over-tightening cracks the toilet base. Snug, not cranked.

### T&P valve (temperature & pressure relief valve)
The safety valve on a water heater (usually on the side or top, with a short downward pipe). Opens automatically if internal pressure or temperature gets dangerous. A small drip from this valve can mean normal operation, excessive pressure, or a failing valve — needs diagnosis, not ignoring.

### Supply line (supply tube)
The braided or rigid tube connecting a fixture (sink, toilet, washing machine, dishwasher) to the home's water supply, via a shut-off valve. A common leak point at the connection nuts. Rubber versions degrade; braided stainless is the modern replacement.

### Shut-off valve (angle stop / stop valve)
The small valve at each fixture that shuts off water to that fixture alone. Usually under a sink or behind a toilet. Critical for isolating a leak without killing water to the whole house — but they seize up from disuse, so exercising them periodically is part of prevention.

### Saddle valve (self-piercing valve)
A cheap clamp-on valve that pierces a supply pipe to feed a fridge icemaker or humidifier. Prone to leaking and clogging; a common source of slow hidden leaks. Modern best practice replaces them with proper tee + ball valves.

### Subfloor
The structural layer under your finished flooring. Slow leaks (toilet wax ring, supply line, tub surround) soak into it silently, and by the time you see damage on the ceiling below, it's often rotted. The expensive hidden cost of ignored leaks.

### Braided supply line (braided stainless)
A flexible supply tube sheathed in woven stainless steel. Far more burst-resistant than rubber — rated to ~1,500 PSI vs. rubber's far lower limit. The modern replacement for rubber washing-machine and fixture hoses. Some versions include an **auto-shutoff** mechanism that seals if the hose bursts.

### Rubber supply hose
The older-style washing machine supply line — plain rubber. Degrades, cracks, and loses flexibility over time, eventually bursting. The #1 cause of catastrophic indoor washing-machine floods. Replace with braided stainless.

### Main shut-off valve
The single valve that stops water to the entire house. Usually near where the main water line enters (basement, crawlspace, utility room) or at the meter. The most important valve to locate and label in your home — it's what you reach for in a flood.

### Ball valve
A modern shut-off with a lever handle — a ¼-turn (90°) opens or closes it fully. Lever parallel to the pipe = open; perpendicular = closed. More reliable than a gate valve, fewer failure modes, and the preferred type for main and fixture shut-offs. Should be operated fully open or fully closed (not left half-open).

### Gate valve
An older shut-off with a round wheel handle — many full turns raise or lower a metal "gate" into the water path. Cheap, so standard on older homes, but prone to seizing, corrosion, and sediment trapping in the gate groove. A common maintenance failure: the valve won't fully close after years of disuse. Modern best practice replaces these with ball valves.

### Exercising a valve
Opening and closing a shut-off valve fully, on a regular schedule (every 6 months), to prevent mineral buildup and seizing. Critical for gate valves especially — a valve that never moves eventually won't move when you need it. Reopen slowly to avoid water hammer.

### Water hammer
A loud bang in the pipes when a valve closes fast, suddenly stopping the water's momentum. Can damage pipe joints and fittings over time. Caused by opening/closing valves too quickly, or by failed air chambers. Reopen valves slowly to avoid it.

### Packing nut (bonnet nut)
The nut just below a valve handle, around the stem. Tightened just enough to stop water leaking out around the stem — over-tightening makes the valve hard to turn. A common cause of a "stuck" valve is an over-tightened packing nut; loosening it slightly often frees the valve.

### Compression fitting
A type of pipe connection (nut + ring/ferrule + seat) used on supply lines to fixtures. Tightened with wrenches — but only hand-tight plus ¼ to ½ turn. A common leak source when over-tightened (cuts the ferrule) or under-tightened.

### Sediment
Minerals (mostly calcium and magnesium) that settle out of heated water and accumulate at the bottom of a water heater tank. Forms a layer that insulates the water from the heat source — wasting energy on gas heaters, risking element burnout on electric ones — and eventually clogs the drain valve. Flushed annually.

### Anode rod (sacrificial anode)
A long metal rod (magnesium, aluminum, or zinc-aluminum) suspended inside a water heater tank. It corrodes *instead of* the tank lining — sacrificing itself so the tank doesn't. When it's consumed, the tank starts corroding. Inspected annually, replaced when &gt;50% gone (cadence depends on type and water chemistry — magnesium ~1–3 yr, aluminum ~3–5 yr). The single biggest factor in how long a tank lasts.

### Dip tube
The pipe inside a water heater that delivers cold incoming water to the *bottom* of the tank, pushing hot water out the top. If it breaks or disintegrates, cold water short-circuits straight to the outlet — you get lukewarm showers despite a "full" tank of hot water.

### Drain valve
The small valve near the bottom of a water heater tank (often plastic, sometimes brass) where you attach a garden hose to flush sediment. Prone to clogging with sediment and to dripping; some homeowners replace the plastic version with a brass ball-valve drain for reliability.

### Heating element (electric only)
The electric resistance coils (usually two: upper and lower) that heat the water in an electric tank. Burn out if powered while the tank isn't full — the #1 way DIYers damage an electric heater during a flush. Never restore power until the tank is refilled and bled.

### Gas valve / control valve (gas only)
The combination gas-control valve on the outside of a gas water heater — regulates gas flow to the burner and houses the thermostat and pilot/integration system. Has settings like OFF / PILOT / ON and a temperature dial. **Working on it = gas work = pro territory** for anything beyond setting the dial.

### Flue / vent (gas only)
The vertical pipe carrying combustion exhaust from a gas water heater up and out of the house. Must be clear and properly drafted, or carbon monoxide enters the living space. Annual visual inspection for disconnection, corrosion, or blockage is homeowner-scope; repair is pro.

### Water heater pan (drain pan)
A shallow metal or plastic pan placed under a water heater (especially indoors or on upper floors) with a drain line running to a safe location. Catches leaks before they become floods. If your heater sits on wood or above a finished space and has no pan, installing one is a worthwhile upgrade.

## Drywall (preview — expanded in the drywall arc)

### Joint compound ("mud")
The gypsum-based paste used to fill joints, cover tape, and patch holes in drywall. Dries hard; sands smooth.

### Drywall tape
Paper or fiberglass-mesh strip embedded in joint compound to bridge joints and cracks so they don't reopen.

## Appliances

### Lint screen (lint filter)
The removable mesh screen inside the dryer, usually pulled from the top of the drum before or after each load. Catches the bulk of lint from the drying clothes. The first line of defense against lint buildup — but it catches only what's airborne in the drum, not what escapes past it into the exhaust path.

### Lint trap cavity
The slot the lint screen slides into. Lint accumulates *past* the screen in this cavity over time, and in the ducting beyond. Periodically vacuumed from inside the drum with a crevice tool, and during a deep clean, accessed from the rear of the machine.

### Check Vent light
A Maytag front-load dryer indicator that illuminates when exhaust airflow is restricted — usually a clogged lint screen or a blocked/long vent run. A persistent Check Vent light means it's time to clean the vent path end-to-end, not just the screen.

### Dryer vent / exhaust vent
The duct path carrying warm, lint-laden air from the dryer, through the wall, to the exterior cap. Usually 4" rigid or semi-rigid metal duct (never vinyl or foil spiral wire — they sag, trap lint, and are a fire hazard). The part most homeowners never clean and the part that lights fires.

### Blower wheel / blower housing
The fan assembly (driven by the dryer motor) that pulls air through the drum and pushes it out the exhaust. Located inside the lower/rear of the cabinet. Lint that gets past the screen accumulates here over years. On the Maytag MED5630HC, the blower wheel is OEM part **687613**.

### Heating element
In an electric dryer like the MED5630HC, the wire coil that heats the air. Located inside the cabinet near the air path. Lint accumulating near it is the fire source — lint is highly combustible and the element runs red-hot.

### Moisture sensor strips
Two metal strips inside the drum (usually near the lint screen) that detect residual moisture in the clothes and tell the dryer when the load is dry. When coated with fabric-softener film, they misread "dry" early and leave clothes damp. Wiped periodically with rubbing alcohol.

### Clean Washer cycle
A dedicated cycle on Maytag front-load washers (the MHW5630HC included) that runs a hot, high-water wash with no clothes — designed to flush residue and mildew from the tub, gasket, and internal passages. Run monthly with an **affresh®** tablet placed directly in the drum (not the dispenser). The machine auto-prompts every 30 loads.

### Pump filter (drain filter)
A accessible screen inside the washer's lower access panel that catches coins, hair, lint, and debris before they reach the drain pump. When it clogs, the washer won't drain or won't spin. Cleaned every few months by unscrewing the cap behind the access panel — have a shallow tray ready for the water that spills.

### Door boot / gasket (washer)
The rubber seal around a front-load washer door. Its folds trap water, hair, and debris — the #1 source of front-load washer odor. Wiped monthly and left to air-dry with the door cracked open between loads.

## Electrical

### Hot / Neutral / Ground
The three conductors in standard home wiring:
- **Hot** (usually black, sometimes red) — carries current *to* the load. Always assume it's live until proven dead.
- **Neutral** (white) — carries current *back* from the load to complete the circuit.
- **Ground** (bare copper or green) — the safety path. Normally carries no current. If a hot wire touches a metal box or appliance chassis, ground gives that fault current a path to trip the breaker instead of electrifying the chassis (and you).

### Circuit
A complete loop from the panel's hot, through the load (outlet, light, appliance), and back to the panel's neutral. "Opening" the circuit (flipping a breaker, pulling a wire) breaks the loop and stops current.

### Circuit breaker
An automatic switch in the panel that trips (opens) to protect wiring from overcurrent. Also the manual switch you flip to cut power before working on a circuit. A single-pole breaker serves one 120V circuit (15A or 20A typically); a double-pole breaker serves 240V circuits (dryer, oven, AC).

### Load (electrical)
Two meanings, both used in this workspace: (1) any device consuming power on a circuit — a light, an outlet feeding an appliance, a fan; (2) the downstream side of a switch or receptacle, as opposed to the "line" (incoming power) side. "Line vs. load" matters when wiring switches, GFCIs, and AFCIs.

### Line (electrical)
The incoming-power side of a device (switch, receptacle, GFCI). Power flows from the panel to the line side, then through the device to the load side. On a GFCI, getting line/load backwards defeats the protection — the terminals are labeled.

### Non-contact voltage tester
A pen-sized tool that beeps and lights when held near a live wire, without touching metal. The first tool you use on any electrical job — <strong>after</strong> flipping the breaker, to confirm the circuit is actually dead. Always test the tester on a known-live circuit first to confirm it works.

### Multimeter
A meter that measures voltage, current, and resistance (and sometimes continuity). More precise than a non-contact tester and the only way to confirm "dead" definitively (a non-contact tester can false-negative if held wrong). For homeowner use, a cheap digital multimeter is sufficient.

### Wire gauge (AWG)
The thickness of the wire, given in American Wire Gauge numbers. Lower number = thicker wire = more current capacity. **14 AWG** is rated for 15A circuits; **12 AWG** for 20A circuits. Never put a higher-amperage breaker on a smaller-gauge wire — that's how fires start.

### GFCI (Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter)
A device (receptacle or breaker) that trips when it senses a mismatch between the current flowing out on hot and returning on neutral — meaning current is leaking somewhere it shouldn't (often through a person). **Protects people from shock.** Required by NEC 210.8 (2020/2023) in: bathrooms, kitchens (all countertop receptacles + dishwasher/disposal circuits), garages, outdoors, laundry, unfinished basements, crawl spaces, accessory buildings. (Pre-2020 code used a "within 6 ft of sinks" trigger; current code is broader.)

### AFCI (Arc Fault Circuit Interrupter)
A device (breaker or receptacle) that detects the electrical signature of a dangerous arc — the sparking that causes electrical fires. **Protects property from fire.** Required by NEC on most 120V, 15A/20A branch circuits in living areas (bedrooms, living rooms, kitchens, etc.). Not required in bathrooms, garages, or outdoors. Modern kitchens require both AFCI and GFCI.

### Pigtail
A short length of wire used to connect two or more circuit wires to a single device terminal. Used when multiple ground wires in a box need to connect to the one ground screw on a switch or receptacle.

### Junction box
The metal or plastic enclosure where wires connect. All wire splices must be inside a junction box with a cover — never buried in a wall or left open. The box protects the connections from damage and contains any arcing.

### Single-pole switch
The standard light switch — one location controls one light. Has two brass terminals (line in, load out) plus a ground screw. Simplest switch type.

### 3-way switch
A switch used in pairs to control one light from two locations (e.g., top and bottom of a staircase). Despite the name, it's not three positions — it's a switching scheme using two switches and a "traveler" wire pair. Wiring is more involved than a single-pole; miswiring is common and the symptom is usually "one switch only works when the other is in the right position."

### Traveler wires (3-way circuits)
The pair of wires (usually red and black, or black and white re-identified) that connect the two 3-way switches. Carrying the switched hot between the two switches so either can complete the circuit.

### Dimmer switch (slider)
A switch that varies voltage to a light to dim it. The visible slider or dial controls the dimming electronics inside. **Important compatibility caveat:** dimmers must match the load type (incandescent, LED, CFL). An LED on a non-LED-rated dimmer flickers or buzzes; an incandescent dimmer on an LED can damage the driver.

### Common terminal (3-way switch)
On a 3-way switch, the single dark-colored screw — distinct from the two brass traveler screws. It connects either to the always-hot feed (at the first switch) or to the switched-hot wire going to the light (at the second switch). The one screw you absolutely must identify correctly when replacing a 3-way; getting it wrong is the most common cause of a 3-way that only works from one position.

### Neutral-required dimmer
A dimmer that needs a white neutral wire in the switch box to power its internal electronics. Most modern LED dimmers require a neutral. Older switch boxes wired without a neutral ("switch loops") can't accept these — you'd need a dimmer specifically rated for no-neutral operation (which uses a small leak current through the bulb, and doesn't work with all LEDs). Check the box for a neutral bundle before buying a dimmer.

### TRIAC dimmer (forward-phase / leading-edge)
The common incandescent/LED dimmer that chops the start of each AC half-cycle. Works with incandescent, halogen, and LEDs whose driver is explicitly TRIAC-compatible (often labeled "forward-phase" or "MLV"). Mismatched with an incompatible LED → flicker, buzz, or low-end cutout. The default home-center dimmer.

### ELV dimmer (reverse-phase / trailing-edge)
A dimmer that chops the end of each AC half-cycle. Better for electronic LED drivers and electronic low-voltage transformers — smoother dimming, less flicker, often requires a neutral wire. Identified on packaging as "ELV," "trailing-edge," or "reverse-phase."

### Receptacle (outlet)
The wall device you plug things into. Technically "receptacle" is the correct term; "outlet" is the broader term for any point where power is taken off the wiring (a light box is an outlet too). Most homeowners say "outlet" — this workspace uses both interchangeably for wall receptacles.

### Duplex receptacle
The standard receptacle with two plug-in slots (top and bottom). Each slot pair is one receptacle; a duplex is two on one strap. The small vertical slot between the blades is the neutral (larger blade); the smaller slot is hot. The round hole below is ground.

### Back-stab (push-in) connection
A termination method where a straight wire is pushed into a spring-loaded hole in the back of a receptacle or switch. <strong>Widely condemned by electricians and inspectors.</strong> The spring contact loosens over time, causes arcing, and is a leading cause of outlet fires. Listed only for 14 AWG (15A) solid copper — never use on 20A circuits. If you find back-stabbed wiring when replacing a device, move the wires to the screw terminals.

### Back-wire (screw-pressure-plate)
A termination method where a straight wire is inserted under a metal plate that is clamped down by tightening the adjacent screw. <strong>Distinct from back-stab and considered reliable.</strong> Listed for both 15A and 20A, and accepted by inspectors. Often used by pros for speed, especially on devices with multiple wires per terminal. Identified by the screw driving a clamping plate, not a spring.

### Side-wire (screw terminal)
The termination method where a wire is bent into a clockwise hook and wrapped under a terminal screw. The long-standing gold standard for reliability — the most defensible connection in an inspection. Used on all devices; the default for 15A and 20A residential work.

### Line vs. load (receptacle wiring)
At a receptacle, power flows from the panel into the box (the <strong>line</strong> side) and may continue onward to the next outlet downstream (the <strong>load</strong> side). On a standard receptacle the two are wired in parallel via pigtails or the break-off tab. On a GFCI, line and load are dedicated, labeled terminals — and getting them backwards defeats the protection.

### Downstream protection
The protection a GFCI receptacle provides to other (regular) receptacles wired from its load terminals further along the circuit. One GFCI at the start of a circuit can protect every outlet after it. This is why a single bathroom or kitchen GFCI often makes several other "regular-looking" outlets safe.

### Break-off tab (receptacle)
The small metal tab connecting the top and bottom receptacle halves on a duplex, allowing them to be split. Present by default (both halves on one circuit). Removed when the two halves are to be wired separately — most commonly for a switched half-hot outlet (one half always on, one switched). Do not remove unless you have a reason.

### Tamper-resistant (TR) receptacle
A receptacle with an internal shutter mechanism that blocks the slots unless both prongs of a plug are inserted simultaneously. Required by NEC since 2008 in all new and replacement receptacles in dwellings. Prevents children from sticking objects into the slots. Slightly stiffer to plug into at first — normal.

## Appliances — refrigerator

### Condenser coils (refrigerator)
The warm external coils that release heat pulled from inside the fridge. Usually at the back or underneath behind a kickplate/grille. Dust coating insulates them and makes the compressor work harder — the rationale for periodic cleaning. On many modern fridges these are sealed or front-accessible and collect less dust than older models.

### Evaporator coils (refrigerator)
The cold internal coils that absorb heat from the fridge interior. Self-defrosting in modern refrigerators — they don't require manual cleaning. A failed defrost cycle shows up as frost buildup on the back wall of the freezer.

### Compressor (refrigerator)
The pump that circulates refrigerant through the sealed system. The heart of the fridge — and the part you're protecting when you clean coils (a hot, dusty compressor works harder and dies sooner). **Refrigerant work on the compressor/sealed system is pro-only** (EPA certification required).

### Kickplate / toe grille
The removable panel at the base of the refrigerator (front or back) that covers the condenser coils and compressor. Snaps off or is held by a few screws — removing it gives access for coil cleaning.

### Door gasket (refrigerator)
The flexible magnetic seal running around the refrigerator and freezer doors. When it fails (cracks, hardens, or loses magnetism), cold air leaks out, the compressor runs constantly, and food spoils faster. Tested with the dollar-bill method; replacement is a DIY job (~$30–60, model-specific).

### Dollar-bill test (gasket seal)
Close the refrigerator door on a dollar bill, then pull it out slowly. Resistance = good seal. Slips out easily = gasket gap. Repeat at several points around each door to find weak spots.

### NSF/ANSI 42 vs. 53 (water filters)
Two certification standards for refrigerator water filters. **NSF 42** covers aesthetic effects (chlorine taste/odor, particulates). **NSF 53** covers health-related contaminants (lead, cysts, VOCs). A filter carrying only NSF 42 improves taste but doesn't protect against lead — relevant if your home has older plumbing.

### Ice maker supply line
The small water line (often braided stainless or plastic tubing) feeding the refrigerator's ice maker and water dispenser. A common leak source — and a flood risk if it fails. Inspect periodically; replace plastic with braided stainless.

## Home envelope & exterior

### Roof flashing
Thin metal (aluminum, galvanized steel, or copper) installed at roof transitions and penetrations to direct water away from seams. Found around chimneys, vent pipes, skylights, valleys, and where roofs meet walls. When flashing rusts, lifts, or loses its sealant, water finds its way under the roof covering — the most common cause of roof leaks, more than the shingles themselves.

### Pipe boot (vent boot)
The rubber or neoprene gasket that seals around a plumbing vent pipe where it exits the roof. The rubber degrades in sun and weather — cracks, splits, and lets water run down the outside of the pipe into the attic. A leading cause of "mysterious" roof leaks in a specific room below a bathroom. Visible from the ground as the black rubber around each white pipe sticking up through the roof.

### Roof valley
The V-shaped internal angle where two roof planes meet — a channel that carries the largest volume of water during rain. Vulnerable to debris buildup, ice dams, and shingle wear. A valley failure leaks at the inside corner of the roof intersection.

### Granule loss (shingles)
The ceramic-coated mineral granules on the surface of asphalt shingles that protect the asphalt from UV. As shingles age, granules slough off and collect in gutters and at downspout exits. Excessive granules in gutters = shingles nearing end of life. Visible from the ground as dark gritty piles.

### Soffit
The horizontal underside of the roof overhang, spanning between the exterior wall and the fascia. Often vented to provide attic intake airflow. Susceptible to rot from roof leaks or condensation; a common entry point for pests (mice, wasps) when damaged.

### Fascia
The vertical board running along the lower edge of the roof, behind the gutter. Mounts the gutters and caps the rafter tails. Rots when gutters overflow and saturate the wood — the most common fascia failure. If gutters are sagging, the fascia behind them may be rotted and no longer holding the spikes/screws.

### Efflorescence
A white, powdery mineral deposit on basement walls (concrete, block, or brick) left when water seeps through the wall and evaporates, carrying dissolved minerals to the surface. Cosmetic itself, but a <strong>red flag for water migration</strong> through the foundation. Near the top of the wall = surface water (grading/gutters); near the bottom = groundwater/high water table.

### Hydrostatic pressure
The outward force water-saturated soil exerts against a foundation wall. The cause of most horizontal foundation cracks and basement seepage. Driven by poor grading, clogged gutters, and downspouts that discharge at the foundation — the same root cause chain behind the user's basement backup.

### Grading (site)
The slope of the ground immediately around the foundation. Correct grading falls <strong>away</strong> from the house — at least 6" drop over the first 10 ft (~5% slope). <strong>Negative grading</strong> (sloping toward the house) directs water to the foundation wall, building hydrostatic pressure. The cheapest, highest-leverage fix in basement-water prevention: add soil to re-establish positive slope.

### Deck ledger board
The horizontal structural board of a deck, bolted to the exterior wall of the house — the connection that holds the deck to the building. The most critical and most-failed deck connection: if water gets behind the ledger (missing/improper flashing), the ledger and the house's band joist behind it rot, and the deck can separate from the house.

### Ledger flashing (Z-flashing)
The metal or membrane flashing that directs water <em>over and away from</em> the deck ledger board, preventing water from running behind it. IRC-required but frequently missing on older decks — the leading cause of ledger rot. Tucks behind the siding/house-wrap above the ledger and laps over its face.

## Garage door

### Torsion spring
The heavy coiled spring mounted on a horizontal shaft directly above the garage door opening. Counterbalances the door's weight so the opener (or a person) can lift it. Under enormous tension — adjustment requires winding bars and precise technique. **Strictly pro-only: stored energy can cause serious injury or death.** Standard lifespan ~10,000 cycles (one cycle = one full open/close); upgraded/oil-tempered springs run 15,000–20,000.

### Extension spring
The older-style springs mounted along the horizontal tracks on either side of the door. Stretch to store energy as the door closes. Generally shorter-lived than torsion springs (~10,000 cycles) and more dangerous when they break — they can snap violently across the garage. Should always have a safety cable running through them. Also pro-only.

### Opener (garage door opener)
The motorized unit (usually ceiling-mounted) that drives the door up and down via a chain, belt, or screw drive. Includes the receiver for remote controls and the logic that runs the safety reversal systems. The opener <em>moves</em> the door; the springs do the actual lifting.

### Auto-reverse (mechanical)
A safety feature that reverses the door if it hits an obstruction while closing. Tested by placing a 2x4 flat on the floor under the door — the door should reverse on contact. Required by federal law on all openers manufactured since 1993.

### Photo eye (photoelectric sensor)
Two sensors mounted near the floor on either side of the door opening, facing each other. One sends an infrared beam, the other receives it. If anything breaks the beam while the door is closing, the door reverses. Required on all openers since 1993. Must be installed no higher than 6 inches off the floor.

### Balance test
A homeowner-safe diagnostic for the springs. With the door closed, disconnect the opener (pull the red release cord), then manually lift the door halfway. A balanced door holds its position; a door that falls or rises on its own needs spring adjustment (pro-only).

### Emergency release cord
The red rope hanging from the opener rail. Pulling it disconnects the door from the opener so the door can be lifted manually — used during a power outage or opener failure. Reconnect by pulling the cord toward the opener and running a cycle.

## Safety devices (smoke & CO)

### Ionization smoke alarm
A smoke detector that uses a tiny radioactive source to ionize air in a sensing chamber; smoke disrupts the ion current and triggers the alarm. Faster at detecting <strong>fast-flaming fires</strong> (grease, paper, flammable liquids). Slower on smoldering fires. Prone to nuisance alarms from cooking — not the best choice near a kitchen.

### Photoelectric smoke alarm
A smoke detector that uses a light beam in a chamber; smoke scatters the light onto a sensor, triggering the alarm. Faster at detecting <strong>smoldering fires</strong> (cigarettes, overheated wiring, upholstery). Fewer nuisance alarms from cooking. The better choice near kitchens and in bedrooms.

### Dual-sensor smoke alarm
A single unit containing both ionization and photoelectric sensors. The NFPA-recommended choice when only one unit per location is practical — catches both flaming and smoldering fires.

### Interconnection (smoke/CO alarms)
A feature where all alarms in a home are linked — wired or wireless — so that when one triggers, <strong>every alarm in the house sounds</strong>. Critical for sleeping occupants in distant rooms who wouldn't otherwise hear a basement or kitchen alarm. Required by NFPA 72 in new construction; wireless interconnection is the retrofit option for existing homes.

### 10-year sealed battery (smoke/CO)
A non-replaceable lithium battery sealed inside the alarm, designed to last the full 10-year life of the detector. Eliminates the battery-replacement chore and the "take the battery out because it's chirping" failure mode. Required in many jurisdictions for battery-only alarms — check local code.

### Carbon monoxide (CO)
An odorless, colorless, toxic gas produced by incomplete combustion. Sources in a home: gas furnace, gas water heater, gas stove, fireplace, attached-garage vehicle exhaust. Cannot be detected by human senses — a CO alarm is the only warning. At high concentrations, incapacitates and kills within minutes.

### CO alarm
A detector that sounds when carbon monoxide reaches dangerous concentrations. Uses an electrochemical sensor that degrades over time — CO alarms have a <strong>shorter lifespan than smoke alarms (typically 7 years)</strong>, even though they look identical. Required near sleeping areas and on every level with fuel-burning appliances or an attached garage.

### Combination smoke + CO alarm
A single unit containing both a smoke sensor and a CO sensor. Saves space and wiring, but the two sensors have different lifespans (smoke 10 yr, CO ~7 yr) — the unit expires at the shorter of the two. Voice-alarm models announce the threat type ("Fire!" vs "Warning, carbon monoxide!") which matters for the different responses each requires.

### Heat detector
A device that triggers on temperature rise, not smoke particles. Used where smoke alarms false-trip — kitchens, garages, dusty or humid areas. Does NOT replace smoke detectors elsewhere; it's a supplement for hostile environments.

## Drywall

### Joint compound ("mud")
The gypsum-based paste used to fill joints, cover tape, and patch holes in drywall. Comes in two fundamentally different curing types (see below). Applied in layers (coats), each allowed to dry/set before the next.

### Drying-type compound (ready-mix / all-purpose)
Joint compound that cures by water evaporation. Comes pre-mixed in a bucket. Shrinks slightly as it dries, so deep fills need multiple coats. Easy to sand; the standard for finish coats. <strong>Not suitable for thick fills or deep gaps</strong> — it cracks and takes too long to dry.

### Setting-type compound ("hot mud")
Joint compound that cures by chemical reaction (like plaster), not evaporation. Comes as a powder you mix with water; hardens in a fixed time (5, 20, 45, 90, 210 minutes). <strong>Does not shrink, can fill deep gaps in one coat, and is much harder than drying-type.</strong> The right choice for first/taping coats and deep fills. Harder to sand; often paired with a drying-type finish coat on top.

### Mesh tape (self-adhesive fiberglass)
A roll of open-weave fiberglass cloth with an adhesive backing. Pressed over a crack or hole, then covered with compound. Easier for beginners than paper tape (no bedding-in step) and good for small repairs. <strong>Must be paired with setting-type compound</strong> — mesh is flexible and can crack under drying-type compound's shrinkage.

### Paper tape (drywall tape)
Non-adhesive paper tape, bedded into a layer of compound with a knife. Creates a stronger, more crack-resistant joint than mesh — the pro choice for seams and corners. Requires the "bed it in and squeeze out the excess" technique; slightly steeper learning curve.

### California patch (hat patch)
A patch technique for doorknob-sized holes. Score the back of a drywall scrap larger than the hole, snap off the gypsum perimeter leaving a paper flap, fit the gypsum core into the hole, and mud the paper flange flat to the wall. Eliminates a separate taping step — the patch's own paper becomes the tape.

### Putty knife / taping knife
The flat-blade tool used to apply and smooth joint compound. A <strong>putty knife</strong> (1–2") is for tiny fills; a <strong>taping knife</strong> (4–6" for fill coats, 8–12" for finish coats) is for larger work. Wider blades feather the compound thinner over a larger area — the key to an invisible patch.

### Feathering
The technique of spreading each coat of compound thinner toward the edges, so the patch blends into the surrounding wall with no visible lip. The goal is a gradual transition the eye (and hand) can't detect — not a flat fill that sits proud of the surface.

### Texture matching
Replicating the wall's existing surface texture (orange peel, knockdown, popcorn, smooth) after a patch, so the repair disappears. The hardest part of drywall work for beginners — an otherwise perfect patch is visible if the texture doesn't match. Spray-can texture products exist for orange peel and knockdown; popcorn comes in aerosol with Styrofoam particles.

## Sump pump

### Sump pump
A pump installed in a pit (the sump basin) in the lowest part of a basement or crawlspace, designed to collect and eject groundwater before it rises to the floor level. Activates automatically via a float switch when water in the pit reaches a set level. The last line of defense against basement flooding — and the most common single point of failure when a basement floods.

### Sump pit (sump basin)
The lined hole in the basement floor where groundwater collects and the sump pump sits. Usually 18–24" across and 2–3 ft deep. Collects water from perimeter drain tiles (footing drains) around the foundation.

### Pedestal sump pump
A sump pump with the motor mounted on a shaft <em>above</em> the pit, only the impeller sitting in the water. Older design — louder, but easier to service and typically longer-lived because the motor isn't submerged. The motor is visible sticking up from the pit cover.

### Submersible sump pump
A sump pump with the motor and impeller integrated into a single unit that sits fully <em>inside</em> the pit, underwater when operating. Quieter, more powerful, and the modern standard. The motor is hidden below the pit cover.

### Float switch
The buoyant trigger in the sump pit that rises with the water level and starts the pump at a set height. The most failure-prone part of a sump pump — it can stick (pump won't start), tangle against the pit wall, or hang up on debris. The homeowner test (pour water, watch the float rise, confirm the pump starts) exists primarily to confirm the float switch works.

### Check valve
The one-way valve on the discharge pipe just above the pump. Prevents water already pushed up the discharge line from flowing back down into the pit when the pump shuts off. Without it, every cycle re-pumps the same column of water — wasting energy and wearing the pump.

### Discharge line
The pipe carrying pumped water from the sump pit, up and out of the house, to a discharge point at least 10–20 ft from the foundation. Must not terminate at the foundation (that water returns to the sump), must not freeze in winter, and must have a clear exterior outlet.

### Battery backup sump pump
A secondary pump installed in the same pit, powered by a 12V deep-cycle battery kept on charge. Activates if the primary pump fails OR if power is lost. Covers two failure modes a single pump can't. Battery needs replacement every 3–5 years.

### Water-powered backup
A secondary pump driven by municipal water pressure (a venturi/ejector) rather than electricity. Runs indefinitely as long as city water is available — unlimited runtime in a long outage. Only viable on municipal water (not wells), and inherently wasteful: uses **~2 gallons of city water per gallon pumped** (varies with supply pressure).

## Dishwasher

### Manual filter (dishwasher)
A removable mesh filter at the bottom of the dishwasher tub that traps food debris. Standard on Bosch, Miele, Samsung, LG, and most modern machines — chosen because it allows the dishwasher to be much quieter (no grinder motor). <strong>Must be removed and rinsed/scrubbed every few weeks</strong> or it clogs, smells, and causes poor cleaning.

### Hard food disposer (dishwasher)
A built-in grinder (e.g., GE's "Piranha") that pulverizes food particles so they flush out with the drain water — no manual filter cleaning needed. Louder than a filter-only machine and being phased out by most manufacturers, but still found on some GE, KitchenAid, and Maytag models. If your dishwasher has one, the filter maintenance step doesn't apply.

### Spray arms (wash arms)
The rotating arms at the bottom (and often middle and top) of the dishwasher tub that spray pressurized water at the dishes. Small jet holes clog with food particles and mineral scale — a top cause of "dishes aren't getting clean on the top rack." Removed by twisting off or pulling a clip; cleared with a toothpick and warm water.

### Rinse aid (rinse agent)
A surfactant additive (filled into a dispenser in the dishwasher door) that reduces water's surface tension so it sheets off dishes instead of beading — dramatically improves drying, prevents spots on glass, and is <strong>required by most modern dishwasher manufacturers</strong> for proper drying performance (especially on Bosch/Miele which don't have a heated dry). Refilled every 1–3 months depending on use.

### High loop (drain hose)
A section of the dishwasher's drain hose routed so it loops up high (typically secured to the underside of the counter) before connecting to the drain or garbage disposer. Prevents dirty sink water from siphoning back into the dishwasher. <strong>Required by plumbing code</strong> in most jurisdictions; missing high loop = recurring odor and contamination.

### Citric acid clean
Running an empty dishwasher cycle with citric acid powder (or a commercial dishwasher cleaner) to dissolve mineral scale from spray arm jets, the heater, and internal passages. The hard-water counterpart to the vinegar/degreaser cycle. Done every 1–3 months depending on water hardness.

## Faucet

### Aerator
The screw-on screen assembly at the tip of a faucet spout. Mixes air into the water stream to maintain pressure while reducing flow — and catches mineral grit before it exits. Clogs with calcium scale over time, causing low/spraying flow at one faucet (not whole-house low pressure). The single most common faucet "repair" — unscrew, soak in vinegar, reinstall.

### Cartridge (faucet)
The internal valve mechanism in a single-handle or two-handle faucet that controls water flow and temperature mixing. The wear part that fails when a faucet drips from the spout or is hard to shut off. Brand-and-model-specific — Moen's 1225/1222 cartridges are standardized across many models; Delta and others vary. Replacement is the standard fix for a dripping handle-style faucet (~$15–35).

### Ceramic disk cartridge
A type of faucet cartridge using two polished ceramic disks that slide against each other to control flow — extremely durable and long-lived (10–20+ years). Found in higher-end and many modern faucets. When a ceramic-disk faucet drips, it's usually a cracked disk or debris between the disks, not general wear — replace the cartridge.

### Compression valve (faucet)
The older two-handle faucet design using a rubber washer pressed against a valve seat to stop flow. The washer wears out and is the part to replace when a compression faucet drips from the spout. Distinct from cartridge faucets — identified by the feel of the handle turning (compression has a multi-turn screw motion; cartridge usually has a ¼-turn or half-turn).

### O-rings (faucet)
Small rubber rings sealing joints inside the faucet — around the spout base, on the cartridge body, at supply connections. When a faucet leaks at the <em>base of the spout</em> (not the tip), worn spout O-rings are the usual cause. Cheap and replaceable, but you have to pull the spout to access them.

### Spout (faucet)
The part the water comes out of. On a pull-out/pull-down kitchen faucet, the spout head detaches from the body on a hose — a common leak point at the hose connection and a feature to note when diagnosing.

## Garbage disposer

### Flywheel (disposer)
The rotating plate at the bottom of the disposer chamber with the impellers (loosely called "blades") that throw food against the grind ring. Driven by the motor. When the disposer jams, the flywheel is stuck — freed by turning the motor shaft from below with an Allen wrench.

### Impellers (disposer)
The lugs on the flywheel that fling food against the stationary grind ring. <strong>Not sharp blades</strong> — disposers grind by centrifugal force and impact, not cutting. This is why they can't handle hard items like bones: there's nothing to slice with.

### Grind ring (shredder ring)
The stationary serrated ring around the inside of the disposer chamber. Food is thrown against it by the impellers and ground between the ring and the flywheel. A worn or corroded grind ring reduces performance — replaceable but usually only worth it on higher-end units.

### Reset button (disposer)
A small red button on the bottom of the disposer unit. Pops out when the motor overheats or draws too much current (typically from a jam). After clearing the jam and letting the motor cool, pressing the reset button restores power. If it won't stay in or trips repeatedly, the motor has failed or the jam isn't fully cleared.

### Hex socket (disposer)
A small hex-shaped socket in the center of the disposer's underside, directly below the motor shaft. A ¼" Allen wrench inserted into this socket lets you manually rock the flywheel back and forth to break a jam — the standard first fix for a humming, non-spinning disposer. Most disposers ship with the wrench taped to the unit; it lives in a small holder on the side.

### FOG (fats, oils, grease)
The collective term for cooking fats that should never go down any drain — kitchen sink or disposer. Liquid when hot, solidify in cold pipes, coat the interior and build up into the line-clogging masses that cause drain backups. See [[Drain & Sewer Clog Prevention|Lesson 0004]] — the same rule applies to the disposer.

### High-loop / air gap (dishwasher drain to disposer)
The point where the dishwasher drain hose connects to the disposer. A high loop (or an air gap fitting) prevents disposer water from flowing back into the dishwasher. Required by code; a missing high loop is a common source of dishwasher odor. See [[Dishwasher Maintenance|Lesson 0022]].

## Hiring a pro

### License (contractor)
A state-issued authorization to perform regulated trade work (plumbing, electrical, HVAC, general contracting). Issued by a state licensing board (e.g., CSLB in California). <strong>Verify it online at the board's website</strong> — don't take the contractor's word or a card. An active license with no complaints is the minimum bar; an expired, suspended, or nonexistent license is a hard stop.

### General liability insurance
Insurance covering property damage or bodily injury caused by the contractor's work. If the pro drops a tool through your roof or a worker damages your foundation, this pays. <strong>Verify via a Certificate of Insurance (COI) sent directly from the insurer</strong> — not a photocopy from the contractor. Minimum coverage should be at least equal to your project value.

### Workers' compensation insurance
Insurance covering injuries to the contractor's employees while on your property. <strong>Without it, an injured worker can sue the homeowner.</strong> Verify via the COI alongside the general liability. Sole proprietors with no employees may be exempt — confirm the exemption is legitimate, not just missing coverage.

### Bond (contractor's bond / surety bond)
A financial guarantee the contractor will complete the work per the contract and code. If the contractor abandons the job, does shoddy work, or doesn't pay subcontractors, you can file a claim against the bond to recover. Typically $5,000–$25,000 depending on state. Verify it's active through the licensing board — it's separate from insurance.

### Certificate of Insurance (COI)
A document from the contractor's insurance broker proving active coverage — listing you (the homeowner) as the certificate holder, with current policy numbers, coverage types, and limits. <strong>Must come from the broker, not the contractor.</strong> A COI more than 30 days old, or from an unlisted insurer, is a red flag.

### Bid (quote / estimate / proposal)
A contractor's written offer to do specified work for a specified price. Three flavors: an <strong>estimate</strong> (rough, non-binding), a <strong>bid</strong> (firm price for a defined scope), and a <strong>proposal</strong> (bid + approach + timeline). For anything beyond a small repair, get <strong>three written bids on the same scope</strong> — not verbal estimates.

### Change order
A written amendment to the contract authorizing work beyond or different from the original scope, with the associated cost. <strong>Never approve verbal change orders</strong> — "while we're in here, we should also…" with no paperwork is how budgets double. Every change order should be signed by both parties <em>before</em> the work is done.

### Lien waiver
A document signed by the contractor (or subcontractor/supplier) acknowledging they've been paid for work or materials, and waiving their right to file a mechanic's lien against your property for that amount. <strong>Collect lien waivers with each payment</strong> — especially the final one — to prevent a subcontractor you never hired from lien-ing your house because the general contractor didn't pay them.

## Winterization

### Frost-free sillcock (freeze-proof faucet)
An outdoor hose bibb designed so the actual valve seat sits <em>inside</em> the heated wall, with only a long empty stem extending through the wall to the outside handle. When closed, water drains out of the stem so nothing freezes. <strong>Still requires the hose to be disconnected</strong> — a hose left attached traps water against the valve and defeats the design. Identified by the long pipe extending ~6–12" into the house behind the handle.

### Hose bibb shutoff (interior)
A separate shut-off valve on the supply pipe feeding an outdoor faucet, located <em>inside</em> the heated envelope (basement, crawlspace, utility room). Closed for winter, with the outdoor faucet left open so the pipe between them drains dry. The standard winterization for non-frost-free faucets. Look for a small valve on the pipe near where it exits the wall.

### Drain-back (winterization)
Opening the outdoor faucet after closing the interior shutoff, so residual water in the exterior pipe runs out instead of freezing. The faucet is left <strong>open</strong> for the winter — a closed valve would trap water and burst the pipe.

### Pipe insulation (foam sleeve / fiberglass wrap)
A closed-cell foam tube or fiberglass wrap fitted around exposed water pipes in unheated spaces (crawlspace, unfinished basement, exterior walls). Slows heat loss so water inside doesn't reach freezing during cold snaps. Cheap (~$1/foot), easy to install, and the primary preventive for pipes in vulnerable locations.

### Heat cable (heat tape)
An electrical cable taped along a vulnerable pipe (or the roof edge) that produces heat when plugged in, preventing freeze-up. Used on pipes that can't be drained or relocated. <strong>Not a substitute for insulation</strong> — a backup for problem pipes, with a finite lifespan and fire risk if old/damaged.

### Ice dam
A ridge of ice that forms at the roof's lower edge (eave) when snowmelt runs down the roof and refreezes at the colder eave. The dam backs up water under the shingles, causing leaks into the attic and walls. <strong>The cause is heat loss from the living space below</strong> — not the weather itself. See [[Winterization|the winterization lesson]].

### Air sealing (attic)
Sealing gaps, cracks, and penetrations in the attic floor (around wiring, plumbing, chimneys, light fixtures, the attic hatch) so warm house air can't leak into the attic and warm the roof deck. <strong>The first and most effective step in ice dam prevention</strong> — insulation alone doesn't stop warm-air leakage. Done with caulk, spray foam, and rigid foam + spray foam at larger penetrations like chimneys.

### Sprinkler blowout
Using a high-volume air compressor to force all water out of an underground irrigation system through the sprinkler heads, preventing freeze damage to buried pipes, valves, and heads. Done in fall before the first hard freeze. Pro service typically $65–100; DIY only with a sufficiently large compressor (most homeowner units lack the CFM).

### Roof melt sock (ice melt sock)
A porous fabric tube filled with ice-melt pellets (calcium chloride), tossed up onto the roof perpendicular to an ice dam to melt a channel through it. A reactive stopgap for an existing dam — not prevention. The permanent fix is the attic air-sealing/insulation/ventilation work that addresses the cause.

## Tools

### Cordless drill/driver
A battery-powered drill used for drilling holes and driving screws. The #1 power tool every homeowner should own. Buy as a kit (drill + battery + charger) first — then future bare tools on the same [[GLOSSARY#Battery platform (cordless tools)|battery platform]] are cheaper.

### Battery platform (cordless tools)
The manufacturer's cordless-tool ecosystem — the battery + charger system that all bare tools in that lineup share. Picking one platform (Ryobi ONE+, DeWalt 20V MAX, Makita 18V LXT, Milwaukee M18) and staying on it makes every future cordless purchase cheaper, because you buy the tool without a second battery. The platform decision matters more than any individual tool's specs.

### Shop vac (wet/dry vacuum)
A heavy-duty vacuum that handles both dry debris and liquids — used for condensate drain clogs, fridge coil cleaning, sump pit cleaning, and post-project cleanup. ~$50 for a homeowner-grade unit. Not a replacement for a household vacuum; a work-site tool.

### Fan-rated box (ceiling)
A junction box specifically listed to support the weight <em>and the dynamic load</em> of a ceiling fan. Stamped "For Fan Support" with a weight rating. Standard light-fixture boxes are <strong>not</strong> fan-rated — they hold a static 50 lb fixture, not a 35 lb spinning fan that torques and vibrates. Installing a fan on a non-rated box is a top cause of fans falling.

### Expansion brace (retrofit fan brace)
A telescoping metal bar that wedges between two ceiling joists to provide a structural mounting point for a fan-rated box, installed from below (no attic access needed). Expanded to fit 16"–24" joist spacing, then screwed into the joists. The standard way to make an existing light-fixture location safe for a fan when the original box isn't fan-rated.

### NEC 35-lb fan rule
NEC provision allowing outlet boxes to support paddle fans up to 35 lb (16 kg) without independent support to a structural member — <em>but only if the box is listed for fan support.</em> Fans over 35 lb require independent support (a brace to a joist, a hanger rod, etc.), regardless of the box's rating.

### Canopy (ceiling fan)
The decorative cover at the ceiling that hides the junction box, wiring connections, and (if present) the remote receiver. Slides up the downrod during installation and seats against the ceiling when the fan is hung.

### Downrod
The metal pipe between the ceiling canopy and the fan motor housing. Length determines blade height — ideal is 7–9 ft from floor to blades. Required on sloped/vaulted ceilings (minimum 12") to keep the fan level and clear of the slope.

### Remote receiver (ceiling fan)
A small module that sits inside the fan's canopy and receives signals from a handheld or wall-mounted remote, then switches power to the fan motor and light independently. Enables fan + light control from a single hot wire at the ceiling — useful when the existing wall switch is a single switch and running a new cable isn't practical.

### 14/3 cable (NM-B)
Nonmetallic sheathed cable with three insulated conductors plus ground: black, red, white, and bare. Used when two switched hots need to run in one cable — the standard for a ceiling fan + light controlled independently by two wall switches. (14/2 has only black + white + ground — one switched hot.)

### Switch leg (ceiling box context)
The cable run from the wall switch up to the ceiling box carrying the switched hot. If a ceiling box has only one switched hot (a single 14/2), the fan and light can't be independently switched without adding a cable or using a remote receiver.
