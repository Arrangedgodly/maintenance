# Condensate Drain Line

> The other homeowner-track [[GLOSSARY#HVAC|HVAC]] task (alongside the [[HVAC Filter Change|filter change]]) — flushing the PVC line that carries [[GLOSSARY#Condensate|condensate]] out of the house before it clogs and floods or silently shuts down the AC.

## The core idea
In cooling mode, water ([[GLOSSARY#Condensate|condensate]]) condenses on the cold [[GLOSSARY#Evaporator coil|evaporator coil]], runs into a [[GLOSSARY#Drain pan|drain pan]], and exits via a PVC [[GLOSSARY#Condensate drain / drain line|drain line]]. The line is dark, wet, and warm — [[GLOSSARY#Algae / biofilm|biofilm]] grows on the walls and slowly clogs it.

**Two failure modes when clogged:**
1. **Flood** — condensate overflows the pan → water damage (no safety switch, or switch broken).
2. **Silent shutdown** — the [[GLOSSARY#Float switch (overflow / safety switch)|float switch]] trips and kills the system to prevent the flood. AC just won't turn on. No error.

## The anatomy (top to bottom)
1. **Evaporator coil** — indoors, in the air handler
2. **Drain pan** — catches condensate
3. **[[GLOSSARY#Cleanout T (T-vent)|Cleanout T]]** — PVC T-fitting with a removable cap. Your access point.
4. **Drain line (PVC)** — exits to floor drain, utility sink, or exterior

The cleanout cap is functional, not decorative — without it, the T can't vent and the line won't drain correctly.

## Signs of a clog
- AC won't turn on (tripped float switch) — check the pan for standing water first
- Water around / dripping from the indoor unit
- Water stains on ceiling/wall below the unit
- Musty smell near the unit
- No water at the line's exit when AC is running (it should drip steadily in cooling mode)

## Routine flush — once per cooling season (+ mid-summer if heavy use)
1. Thermostat OFF.
2. Find the [[GLOSSARY#Cleanout T (T-vent)|cleanout T]] near the indoor unit. Remove the cap.
3. Look down the line — standing water = already clogged.
4. Pour **1 cup distilled white vinegar**. Wait 30 min.
5. Flush with **1 gallon warm water**. Should drain freely. Backs up = clogged.
6. Replace the cap.
7. Restore thermostat.

## Bleach vs. vinegar
| | Vinegar | Bleach (diluted 50/50) |
|---|---|---|
| Kills biofilm | Yes, gentler | Yes, stronger |
| Corrosion risk | Very low | Can corrode metal components & rubber seals |
| Best for | **Routine prevention** | Stubborn clog, PVC-only lines |

**Default to vinegar.** Never mix bleach with anything but water; flush thoroughly after.

## Clearing an actual clog (escalating)
1. **Wet/dry shop vac from the outside end** — seal hose to the exit point (floor drain / sink / exterior), pull the clog *out*. Usually fastest.
2. Repeat vinegar + hot water flush, longer soak (1 hr).
3. Flexible brush or zip-tie down the cleanout to break up biofilm. Nothing sharp.
4. Compressed air / small water bladder sized for ¾" PVC. Gentle pressure.

**Call a pro if:** clog won't clear, cracked drain pan (needs replacement), or water has already damaged surrounding structure.

## The float switch
A safety device in/on the drain pan. Backed-up condensate lifts a float → switch opens → shuts down compressor + condenser fan + supply fan to prevent a flood. If your system lacks one, it's a worthwhile add — converts a flood into a shutdown. Have a pro install it at the next tune-up.

## DIY or pro?
**DIY for routine flush + clearing.** **Pro** for cracked pan, persistent clogs, float-switch installation, or any structural water damage.
